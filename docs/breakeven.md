# Road vs rail break-even per era (with capacity limits)

Generated by `python -m tf3planner.capacity` (function `capacity.breakeven_table(game, vehicles, year)`)
from the live export: real vehicles from the catalogue (base game and mods), coal (BULK), industry to
industry (so there is no delivery-time limit), route = 1.25 × straight distance, and new road/track
(`economics.evaluate_link` prices it with `cheapest_way`). Every vehicle type is evaluated separately
(`capacity.best_with_capacity`) and the best one is kept **after** the capacity rules. That matters
because the unconstrained model favours tiny trucks.

Score = annual profit − capex × CRF(10 %, 20 y), in M$/yr, the same metric as the
real_logistics.md §8.2 sweep. A game year is 1461 s, so a 1900 train makes fewer than one round
trip per year on 12 km. All capacities below are per game year.

## Capacity rules applied (see `tf3planner/capacity.py` docstring)

| Rule | Value | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Loading rate | `loadSpeed × cargo.loadSpeedFactor (0.0625) × platform (2 spec / 1 / 0.25 wrong) × stock (2 spec. warehouse)` units/s per vehicle; wagons load in parallel; overlength part ×0.25 | Model from the scripts; the absolute scale is **unverified (Q5)** |
| Terminal occupancy | ρ = n · dwell / trip; terminals = ⌈ρ / 0.7⌉; dwell = load + 4 s stop delays + manoeuvre (road 6 s, rail 20 s) | UIC 406 / §10.1; manoeuvre times are assumed |
| Truck station | ≤ 8 bays, ≤ 4 entrance/exit pairs (4 s per vehicle per gate) | Assumed |
| Road lane | q = 0.6 · v / (10 m + 3 m + 1.5 s · v); v/c ≤ 0.85; BPR speed factor 1/(1 + 0.15 · s · x⁴), with s = trafficSpeedSensitivityScale | Assumed (Q9) |
| Road fleet cap | ≤ 150 trucks per line | **Practical assumption**; it decides most post-1950 cells (see the sensitivity tables) |
| Single track | cap = m · 0.7 · Y / (2 · (L/(k+1)/v_eff + c + (m−1)·h)), c = train_len/v + 15 s + v/(2·0.25); loops ≥ 1 km apart; one platoon (m = 1..3) per section | UIC 405/406 + TF3 path signals; unverified (Q9) |
| Double track | h = (300 m + train_len)/v_eff + 10 s, cap = 0.7 · Y / h | Assumed block length |
| Loop cost | (m · train_len + 40 m + 2 × 40 m) × track $/m | Signals are not priced (not in export) |
| Maintenance | 1 depot (needed to buy vehicles) + the cheaper of maintenance buildings or depots to cover the pool (road 12/16, rail 6/12) | The pool is assumed to count assigned vehicles |

## Conclusions

The tables give the smallest volume (u/yr) at which rail wins, per distance:

| Era | 3 km | 6 km | 12 km | 24 km | 48 km | Without the 150-truck cap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1900 | 1000 | 1000 | 300 | 300 | 100 | 1000 at every distance |
| 1925 | 3000 | 3000 | 1000 | 1000 | 300 | 3000 only |
| 1950 | never | 3000 | 3000 | 1000 | 1000 | never |
| 1975 | never | never | never | 3000 | 1000 | never |
| 2000 | never | never | never | 3000 | 3000 | never |

1. **1900 (horse carts at 6 units and 20–25 km/h vs 1900 steam).** Rail wins from about
   300 u/yr above 12 km, and from 100 u/yr at 48 km. Below about 300 u/yr and 12 km, carts win.
   **At 3000 u/yr no single line is feasible in either mode.** It would need 34 trains/yr each way
   against about 20 that a double track carries with 12-wagon trains, or 1,359+ carts. Split such
   a flow into parallel lines, or allow longer trains: economics.py caps trains at 12 wagons,
   while the platform allows up to 840 m.
2. **1925 (12-unit trucks at 30–40 km/h).** Road takes everything up to 300 u/yr below 48 km.
   Rail wins at ≥1000 u/yr from 12 km, and at 3000 u/yr at any distance.
3. **1950–2000.** Without the fleet cap, the model says **trucks always win**: road profit per
   unit stays higher, and bays and road v/c only bind at about 3000 u/yr over 3–6 km. With the
   cap, rail takes only the heavy, long legs (≥1000–3000 u/yr at ≥24 km). The case for rail after
   1950 therefore rests on a game limit that has not been measured: how many trucks one road and
   one station really sustain. **Calibrate this first (Q9 and truck station queueing).**
4. **Loading model.** The 16× difference between the loadSpeedFactor model and the economics.py
   model changes only 3 of 125 cells: 1925 3 km/3000 and 1950 6 km/3000 go to road, and
   1975 12 km/3000 goes to rail. Trucks stay at ≤ 8 bays until about 3000 u/yr. The loading scale
   still matters for fleet size and bay counts, so measure Q5.
5. **Track layout.** With more than one train on a line, single track needs passing loops at
   roughly 1–3 km spacing. At 50 km/h with 180 m trains, single track saturates at about
   6.4 trains/yr each way, whatever the line length, because loops cannot be closer than 1 km.
   A double track with 300 m blocks carries about 20. Most ≥1000 u/yr rail cells end up
   double-track.
6. **2000-era rail is penalised by the default track.** `routing.default_way` picks the cheapest
   plain track ("simple", 100 km/h), so 140–200 km/h locomotives run at 100 km/h. Locomotives cost
   10–25 M$. Re-run with `standard`/`high_speed` track before concluding that late rail is dead.
7. **Map volumes are 20–330 u/yr per industry**, so after 1925 single producers belong on trucks.
   Rail needs consolidation: several producers on one trunk, or long legs.

Legend for the per-era tables: the cell shows the winner with capacity rules, with the
unconstrained winner of `evaluate_link` in brackets. In the score table, `x` marks an infeasible
option, `T` is trucks, `tr` is trains, `kL` means k passing loops and `2T` means double track.
`none` means neither mode fits on one line.
